This page puts three numbers in front of you: how fast forests are being cut, how fast glaciers are melting, and how many species are in danger. The figures come from the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the World Resources Institute and Global Forest Watch, the IUCN Red List, and a large international glacier study. All of them are estimates, and they change every year, so the date of each figure is shown.

1. Forests

4.14 billion haforest left, about 32% of all land (FAO, 2025)
10.9 million hacleared every year, 2015 to 2025 (FAO)
6.7 million hatropical primary forest lost in 2024 alone (Global Forest Watch)

There is some good news here. The FAO says deforestation has slowed. In the 1990s about 17.6 million hectares were cleared each year, and in 2015 to 2025 it was about 10.9 million. Forests also regrow and are planted, so the net loss is smaller, about 4.12 million hectares a year, down from about 10.7 million in the 1990s.

Cleared per year, 1990 to 2000: 17.6 million ha
Cleared per year, 2015 to 2025: 10.9 million ha
Net loss per year, 2015 to 2025: 4.12 million ha

The bad news is that 2024 was the worst year on record for tropical primary forest, the oldest and richest kind, which cannot simply be replaced by planting. Global Forest Watch measured 6.7 million hectares lost, almost twice the 2023 figure, and for the first time fire, not farming, was the leading cause, close to half of the loss. Those fires released about 4.1 gigatonnes of greenhouse gases.

Why two different numbers? FAO counts all forest and includes planting and regrowth. Global Forest Watch tracks tree-cover loss from satellites, and some of it is temporary. They measure different things, so do not compare them directly.

2. Glaciers

273 billion tof ice lost per year on average, 2000 to 2023
about 5%of all glacier ice lost since 2000
+36%faster loss in 2012 to 2023 than in 2000 to 2011

These numbers come from a community study that combined many teams' measurements from satellites and field surveys. Regions vary a lot: some have lost only 2% of their ice since 2000, while others have lost nearly 40%. The study found that glaciers have lost more mass than the Greenland ice sheet (about 18% more) and more than double the Antarctic ice sheet.

Why does it matter? Glacier meltwater raises sea levels, and in places like the Himalaya and the Andes, rivers fed by glaciers supply drinking water and farming for hundreds of millions of people.

3. Species

175,909species assessed by the IUCN Red List (version 2026-1)
49,500+of them threatened with extinction
28%of the assessed species

Please read this one carefully. The 28% does not mean that 28% of all life on Earth is threatened. Scientists have described around two million species, and the Red List has assessed only a part of them, often the ones people already worry about. The real share could be higher or lower. What the number does tell us is that tens of thousands of known species are close to disappearing, and that we are only beginning to know the full picture.

Extinction numbers are even harder. Declaring a species extinct needs years of failed searches, so the official count always lags behind reality.

What you can do

I will be honest: one person's choices are small next to what governments and big companies do. But small things add up, and they change what people around you think is normal.

  • Waste less food. Growing food is the largest cause of forest loss, and wasted food is land cleared for nothing.
  • Use less, repair more, buy fewer new things. Everything made takes land, water and energy.
  • Cut the biggest energy habits first: long flights, a car where a bus or bicycle will do, and heating or cooling that is not needed.
  • Eat less beef and dairy if you can. Cattle are a major reason for forest clearing in many countries. You do not have to be perfect. Even a few meals a week helps.
  • Plant native trees and protect the wetlands, ponds and old trees in your own area. Native plants support native insects and birds.
  • Never release pets or plants into the wild and do not buy wild animals or products made from them.
  • Use your voice. Vote, sign petitions, support local groups protecting forests and rivers, and talk about it with friends and family.
