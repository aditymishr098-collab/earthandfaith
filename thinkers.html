--- layout: page title: "Thinkers Who Questioned Belief" permalink: /thinkers/ ---

People have questioned religion, superstition and unfair traditions for thousands of years, in India and everywhere else. Here are nine of them. For each one I note what they said, where I think they were right, and where you should be careful. None of them was perfect, and I do not want anyone on this site to be treated like a saint.

Not every person here was an atheist. Some questioned caste, some questioned superstition, some questioned only parts of religion. I have said so in each case.

Charvaka (Lokayata)

Ancient Indian school of thought, roughly 600 BCE or earlier. Founder traditionally named as Brihaspati.

What they said

Only what we can perceive is real. There is no soul that outlives the body, no heaven or hell, and rituals for the dead do nothing. One line often attributed to them says to live happily while life lasts.

Where they were right

They insisted on evidence at a time when ritual and authority ruled, which shows that doubt is not a foreign import to India.

Be careful

Their own books are lost. We know them mostly through critics who wanted to refute them, so the picture may be unfair or incomplete.

Jyotirao and Savitribai Phule

Jyotirao (1827 to 1890) and Savitribai (1831 to 1897), Maharashtra. Not atheists.

What they said

Caste was a human invention dressed up as religion. Jyotirao founded the Satyashodhak Samaj in 1873 to challenge priestly control, and wrote Gulamgiri the same year. Together they opened schools for girls and for lower-caste children, which was a radical thing to do.

Where they were right

Education and equality did more for people than any argument about God. They proved it by doing the work, not just by writing.

Be careful

Their historical theories, such as the story of an Aryan conquest, are products of the 19th century and are debated by historians today.

Bertrand Russell

1872 to 1970, Britain. Philosopher and mathematician. Called himself an atheist in some moods and an agnostic in others.

What he said

In the 1927 lecture Why I Am Not a Christian he argued that the classic arguments for God fail, and that fear and the wish for comfort explain much of belief. His famous image is a teapot orbiting the Sun that nobody can disprove, which makes the point that the burden of proof lies on whoever makes the claim.

Where he was right

The burden-of-proof idea is a useful tool in everyday life, not only in religion.

Be careful

His targets were mostly Christian ideas and the Church of his time. Other traditions deserve their own examination. He was also sometimes wrong in politics. For example, his views on nuclear weapons changed more than once.

Carl Sagan

1934 to 1996, United States. Astronomer and science writer. Described himself as agnostic.

What he said

Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. In The Demon-Haunted World (1995) he argued for a "baloney detection kit": check the source, ask for evidence, and be ready to be wrong. His Pale Blue Dot reflection (1990) asks us to be gentler with each other, because we share one small planet.

Where he was right

He was respectful toward people while firm against bad evidence. That is the tone I try to follow here.

Be careful

Being a great scientist does not make every prediction right. He warned in 1991 that the oil fires in Kuwait could have major global effects, and that did not happen. Also, a belief that life exists elsewhere is a hope, not evidence.

Periyar E. V. Ramasamy

1879 to 1973, Tamil Nadu. Social reformer and rationalist, open atheist.

What he said

He launched the Self-Respect Movement in 1925. He fought caste hierarchy, temple entry bans and child marriage, supported widow remarriage and women's education, and argued that blind belief keeps people unequal. He also took part in the 1924 Vaikom protest for the right of lower-caste people to use the roads near a temple.

Where he was right

Many of his demands, such as women's rights, temple entry and caste equality, are now accepted basics. He changed a whole region's social thinking.

Be careful

His language was often harsh, and some of it was aimed at whole communities, not only at ideas. I do not follow that style on this site. Some of his later positions, such as on Tamil language and separate Dravidian politics, are still debated.

B. R. Ambedkar

1891 to 1956, India. Jurist, chair of the committee that drafted the Constitution. Not an atheist.

What he said

In Annihilation of Caste (1936) he argued that caste cannot be fixed without rejecting the scriptures that support it. After years of searching, he converted to Buddhism on 14 October 1956 in Nagpur, along with a very large crowd of followers, rejecting what he saw as the unequal order of Hindu society. He saw Buddhism as a religion based on reason and compassion.

Where he was right

He showed that the question is not only "is there a God" but "who is treated as lesser because of belief".

Be careful

Do not file him under "atheist". He believed that society needs some moral foundation, and he chose a religion for it. Scholars still debate his reading of Buddhism.

Bhagat Singh

1907 to 1931, Punjab. Revolutionary against British rule, executed at 23. Atheist.

What he said

In 1930, in Lahore jail, he wrote the essay Why I Am an Atheist. He said he did not believe in God because he could not explain suffering and injustice as God's will, and that he refused to turn to prayer in his hardest hour, as that would be weakness.

Where he was right

His essay shows that disbelief can come from honest thought and courage, not from arrogance. He also warned against blind hero worship.

Be careful

He believed in revolutionary violence and took part in the killing of a British police officer in 1928. This site does not support that. Admire the courage of his thinking, not the violence.

Gora (Goparaju Ramachandra Rao)

1902 to 1975, Andhra Pradesh. Social reformer and atheist.

What he said

He and his wife Saraswathi founded the Atheist Centre in 1940 and used atheism for practical work: fighting untouchability, supporting inter-caste marriages, helping poor villagers and working for peace. He met Gandhi and exchanged ideas with him.

Where he was right

He showed that a non-believer can lead a life of service, not just argument.

Be careful

Because the movement grew around one family and one centre, much of what we know comes from its own writings. Look for independent accounts as well.

Narendra Dabholkar

1945 to 2013, Maharashtra. Doctor and campaigner against superstition.

What he said

He founded a group in 1989 to expose fake godmen, black magic and "miracle cures" that take money and sometimes lives from poor people. He spent years pushing for a law against such exploitation. On 20 August 2013 he was shot dead while on a morning walk in Pune. A few months later Maharashtra passed an anti-superstition law, in December 2013.

Where he was right

He drew a clear line: the target is exploitation, not a person's prayer. He is reported to have said that he was not against belief in God.

Be careful

The law covers harmful practices and does not settle the wider debate on where "tradition" ends and "superstition" starts. That line is argued over in good faith.
