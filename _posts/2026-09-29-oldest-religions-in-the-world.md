Ask ten people which is the oldest religion in the world and you will probably get ten different answers. Hinduism. Judaism. Something in Africa. Something in a cave. The truth is a little messier, and a lot more interesting, than a simple top-five list.

## First, a problem with the question

Before we go anywhere, we have to decide what "oldest" means, because it can mean at least three different things.

It could mean the **oldest signs of religious behavior**, like a burial with gifts for the dead. It could mean the **oldest religion we can read about**, meaning one that left written texts. Or it could mean the **oldest religion still practiced today** in a line that has never quite broken.

These give very different answers. And there is a fourth issue that nobody can get around: religion existed for tens of thousands of years before anyone wrote anything down. So when we say "oldest", we are always guessing from bones, tools, paintings and stories. Honest historians will tell you that up front. So will I.

## The deep past: burials, caves and a hill in Turkey

Around 100,000 years ago, at a place called Qafzeh in what is now Israel, early modern humans buried their dead. Some of the graves contained red ochre and seashells. Did that mean these people believed in an afterlife? Maybe. Some researchers think so. Others point out that we cannot read minds from a pile of shells, and they are right to be careful.

Then there is art. The lion-headed carving from Hohlenstein-Stadel in Germany, a figure with a human body and a lion's head, is around 40,000 years old. The paintings in Chauvet Cave in France are roughly 36,000 years old. Nobody knows exactly why they were made, but many scholars suspect ritual, storytelling or something we would now call spiritual.

The biggest surprise came from Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Turkey, dated to roughly 9500 BCE. Huge carved stone pillars, some decorated with animals, were set up in circles, and this happened before farming or pottery. That upset an old idea that cities and farms came first and temples followed. At Göbekli Tepe, it looks like people may have gathered for something sacred before they ever settled down. Even so, we do not really know what they did there. The site has no writing to explain itself.

## Living traditions that reach very far back

Some religions today have roots that people claim go back tens of thousands of years. These claims are exciting, but they need care.

**Aboriginal Australian traditions.** Humans have lived in Australia for at least 50,000 years, and the stories and ceremonies of Aboriginal peoples, often grouped under the English word "Dreaming", are still alive. There is a fascinating study from 2016 by researchers Patrick Nunn and Nicholas Reid. They looked at stories from coastlines across Australia that describe the sea rising and covering land, and they argued that some of these stories may keep memories of real events from more than 7,000 years ago. Not everyone agrees on how far back oral tradition can go, but it is a striking idea.

**The San of southern Africa.** The San are among the oldest continuing human populations on Earth, according to genetic studies. Their traditions, including trance dances and rich rock art, have been documented for a very long time. One researcher, Sheila Coulson, has claimed that a cave at Tsodilo Hills in Botswana shows python-related ritual about 70,000 years ago. That claim is disputed, so it is best treated as an open question.

The honest lesson is that many Indigenous religions are very old, but they are also living, changing traditions. Being old does not mean being frozen.

## The oldest religions we can actually read about

Writing changes everything, because for the first time people can speak to us directly.

**Sumerian religion (Mesopotamia).** Writing appeared in Sumer around 3400 to 3000 BCE, and religion was part of it from the start. The Sumerians had gods for the sky, the earth, water and the harvest, and they built temples at the center of their cities. Their myths, later copied by the Babylonians, include early versions of flood stories.

**Ancient Egyptian religion.** Egypt's religious texts are some of the oldest large collections we have. The Pyramid Texts, carved inside royal pyramids around 2400 to 2300 BCE, are spells and hymns meant to help a dead king reach the afterlife. Egyptian religion lasted for about three thousand years, longer than the time between us and the Roman Empire, before it faded under Roman and then Christian and Islamic rule.

**Chinese ancestor worship and early folk religion.** Some of the earliest Chinese writing, cut into "oracle bones" during the Shang dynasty (around 1250 BCE onward), was used to ask ancestors and spirits questions about the harvest, war and the weather. Ancestor veneration is still a living part of Chinese culture today.

## Old religions that are still practiced

Now the question most people really mean: which old religions are still around?

**Hinduism.** Many people call it the oldest major religion still alive. Its earliest texts, the hymns of the Rigveda, were composed around 1500 to 1200 BCE and passed down by memory for a long time before they were written. That gives Hinduism roots at least 3,000 years deep. But there is a caution here. Hinduism as we know it grew and changed enormously over those centuries, absorbing new philosophies and practices. Many Hindus themselves prefer the term "Sanatana Dharma", meaning something like "eternal way", and see it as beyond any starting date.

**Judaism.** The first known mention of "Israel" outside the Bible is on an Egyptian monument from about 1208 BCE. Jewish religion developed over the following centuries, and many of its texts were gathered and edited between roughly 800 and 400 BCE. It is one of the oldest monotheistic traditions and the parent of Christianity and Islam in many ways.

**Zoroastrianism.** Founded on the teachings of the prophet Zoroaster, it is one of the oldest religions built around a good-versus-evil worldview. Scholars cannot agree on when he lived. Some say around 1500 to 1000 BCE, others as late as 600 BCE. It was once the state religion of the Persian empires, but today it is a small community, well under 200,000 people worldwide, with the biggest groups in India (the Parsis) and Iran.

**Other very old living traditions** include Shinto in Japan, whose practices reach back into prehistory even though its first written records come from the 700s CE, Chinese folk religion, and countless Indigenous religions across every continent.

## So which one is the oldest?

Here is the honest answer, laid out simply.

| Question | Best answer |
|---|---|
| Oldest signs of religious behavior | Burials and cave art from tens of thousands of years ago, meaning debated |
| Oldest site that may be sacred | Göbekli Tepe, around 9500 BCE |
| Oldest religion we can read about | Sumerian and Egyptian religion, over 4,000 years ago |
| Oldest major religion still practiced | Hinduism, with roots of about 3,000 years or more |
| Oldest living Indigenous traditions | Aboriginal Australian and San traditions, but exact ages are debated |

## Why this matters

I find one thing in all this very humbling. The oldest religions were not created by a founder who woke up one morning with a new idea. They seem to have grown slowly, out of fear of death, wonder at the sky, and the need to explain why the rain comes or does not. People buried their dead with care for tens of thousands of years before anyone wrote the word "god".

It also reminds us that every religion alive today, however ancient it feels, is a moment in a very long story. Some have lasted for millennia. Others, like the religion of ancient Egypt, lasted longer than almost anything we can imagine and still vanished. That should make us curious, and a little humble, about how any belief system begins, spreads and fades.
