This is the first post in a new series where I look at one country at a time: how religion arrived there, what happened because of it, what people claim about it, and where things stand today. I am going through the countries in the order of their international phone codes, which is a slightly silly rule but a fair one, because it stops me from choosing only the countries I find easy. The first code is +1, and that means the United States.

The same rules as always apply. I criticise ideas, not people. I give numbers where I can, and I say plainly when they are rough. And I check claims against the same standard for every religion, including the ones I was never taught myself.

## The people who were already there

When Europeans arrived, the land was not empty. Hundreds of Native nations lived across what is now the United States, each with its own beliefs, stories and ceremonies. There was no single &ldquo;Native American religion&rdquo;. Many of these traditions tied the sacred to particular rivers, mountains and animals, which is one reason forced removal from the land was also an attack on religion.

What came next was harsh. Christian missionaries were part of the colonial project, and some federal and church-run boarding schools tried to erase Native languages and spiritual practices from children. In 1890, at Wounded Knee in South Dakota, US soldiers killed around 250 to 300 Lakota people, many of them unarmed, after fear spread about the Ghost Dance, a spiritual movement. Native religious practice was effectively restricted by law for a long time, and it was only in 1978 that Congress passed the American Indian Religious Freedom Act.

## Refuge for some, danger for others

The popular story says the Pilgrims and Puritans sailed to America for religious freedom. That is true, but incomplete. They wanted freedom for *their own* way of worship, and they were not keen to share it.

In the Massachusetts Bay Colony, you could be fined, whipped or banished for the wrong beliefs. Roger Williams was banished in 1635 for arguing that the government should not control conscience, and he went on to found Rhode Island, which became a refuge. Between 1659 and 1661, the colony hanged four Quakers, among them Mary Dyer, for returning after being ordered to leave. Other Quakers were whipped and banished. Maryland passed a Toleration Act in 1649, which sounds modern until you read the details: it protected only Trinitarian Christians, and denying the Trinity could still carry the death penalty.

## Salem, 1692

Salem is the best-known case of religious panic in American history. Between February 1692 and May 1693, more than 200 people were accused of witchcraft. Nineteen were hanged, one man, Giles Corey, was pressed to death for refusing to enter a plea, and at least five more died in jail. The courts later called the trials unlawful. Convictions were reversed for many of the victims in 1711, and the last person convicted, Elizabeth Johnson Jr., was formally exonerated in 2022, 329 years after her conviction.

Compared with Europe, where about 40,000 to 60,000 people were executed for witchcraft over three centuries, Salem was small in numbers. But it shows how quickly fear, belief in the devil and the courts can combine into something deadly.

## The founding: a country that tried to keep religion and government apart

The Americans who wrote the early laws had seen the old European model, in which the state picked a church and punished the others. Many of them wanted to avoid it, though for different reasons. Some, like Jefferson and Madison, thought government should stay out of conscience altogether. Others, like many evangelical Baptists, thought government interference corrupted the church.

- **Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom (1786):** written by Jefferson, it said no one could be forced to support or attend any church.
- **Article VI of the Constitution (1788):** no religious test for any public office.
- **First Amendment (1791):** Congress cannot establish a religion, and cannot ban the free exercise of religion.

The Constitution never mentions God or Christianity, apart from a routine date at the end (&ldquo;in the Year of our Lord&rdquo;). In 1797 the US Senate unanimously ratified a treaty with Tripoli whose Article 11 says that the government of the United States &ldquo;is not in any sense founded on the Christian religion&rdquo;. Historians argue about how much weight to put on that sentence, since it was a diplomatic line written to reassure a Muslim ruler. But it shows that such a statement could pass without a fight.

## The 1800s: revivals, slavery and fear of newcomers

The country saw waves of intense religious revival, known as the Great Awakenings, and new churches sprang up quickly. Religion was on both sides of the biggest moral argument of the century. Abolitionists, many of them devout Christians, used the Bible to argue against slavery. Slaveholders used it too, to defend it. The Southern Baptist Convention was founded in 1845 after a split over whether slaveholders could be missionaries, and it formally apologised for its racist history only in 1995. Enslaved people, in turn, built their own churches and found strength and hope in them, and that tradition later fed the civil rights movement.

Some newer religions had a rough start:

- **Catholics** faced a wave of anti-Catholic feeling when Irish and German immigrants arrived. A convent in Massachusetts was burned in 1834, and riots in Philadelphia in 1844 killed more than a dozen people and burned churches.
- **Mormons** were driven from state to state after their church was founded in 1830. In 1838 Missouri's governor issued an order saying Mormons must be &ldquo;exterminated or driven from the state&rdquo;, and about two dozen of them were killed in the following weeks, 17 of them at Haun's Mill. In 1844 a mob killed their founder, Joseph Smith, in Illinois.
- **Mormons were also perpetrators.** In September 1857, in Utah, members of the Mormon community and local militia killed at least 120 members of a wagon train at Mountain Meadows. Historians still debate how far up the chain of command responsibility went. The LDS Church expressed &ldquo;profound regret&rdquo; in 2007.

## The 1900s: science, civil rights and cults

In 1925, a Tennessee teacher, John Scopes, was put on trial for teaching evolution. It was a symbolic fight over whether religion should decide what is taught in public schools, and similar fights went on for a century, ending in the 2005 Kitzmiller case, where a federal court ruled that &ldquo;intelligent design&rdquo; was religion and not science.

At the same time, churches were at the centre of the civil rights movement. Black churches organised marches and gave the movement leaders such as Martin Luther King Jr. But they were also targets: in 1963 a bomb at a Black church in Birmingham, Alabama killed four girls.

America also produced some of the most disturbing cult tragedies. In 1978, 918 people, including many children, died in Jonestown, Guyana, under the leadership of Jim Jones, an American preacher. In 1993 the siege at Waco in Texas ended with the deaths of 82 Branch Davidians, including 28 children, plus four federal agents killed in the first raid. In 1997, 39 members of Heaven's Gate died together in California.

## What religion has cost in lives in the US

America never had a European-style religious war between rival states. What it had was a series of episodes. The table is a summary, and the numbers are estimates:

| Event | Years | Deaths (approx.) | How big was religion's role? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quakers hanged in Massachusetts | 1659 to 1661 | 4 | Primary |
| Salem witch trials | 1692 to 1693 | 25 or more (20 executed, 5+ in jail) | Primary |
| Mormon conflicts in Missouri and Illinois | 1838 to 1844 | dozens | Mixed (religion, politics, land) |
| Mountain Meadows massacre | 1857 | at least 120 | Mixed (religion, war fears, land) |
| Wounded Knee | 1890 | about 250 to 300 | Mixed (conquest; fear of Ghost Dance) |
| Birmingham church bombing | 1963 | 4 | Race was the motive; a church was the target |
| Jonestown | 1978 | 918 | Cult led by one man |
| Waco | 1993 | 82 Davidians and 4 agents | Cult plus a failed government raid |
| 9/11 attacks | 2001 | 2,977 | Religious extremism as justification |
| Tree of Life synagogue, Pittsburgh | 2018 | 11 | Antisemitic hatred |

For a country of 330 million people over 400 years, the number of deaths from religious violence is much lower than in Europe's religious wars. Many historians argue that is partly because the founders refused to give any church control of the state, and because the country was so religiously mixed that no single group could dominate for long. That is not proof, but it is a reasonable idea worth taking seriously.

## Claims and what the evidence says

**&ldquo;America was founded as a Christian nation.&rdquo;**
Most of the founders were Christians of some kind, and some, like Jefferson and Franklin, were closer to deists. But the Constitution does not mention God or Christianity in its rules, bans religious tests for office, and the Senate unanimously approved a treaty that says the country is not founded on the Christian religion. The motto &ldquo;In God We Trust&rdquo; was put on a coin in 1864 and became the national motto only in 1956, and &ldquo;under God&rdquo; was added to the Pledge of Allegiance in 1954. So the claim is better described as: a country with a mostly Christian population, whose founding law is deliberately neutral.

**&ldquo;Separation of church and state is not in the Constitution.&rdquo;**
The exact phrase is not there. It comes from Jefferson's 1802 letter to the Danbury Baptists. But the First Amendment's ban on establishing a religion has been read by courts, for a long time, as creating that separation. Americans still argue about how strict it should be.

**&ldquo;The Pilgrims and Puritans came for religious freedom.&rdquo;**
They came for freedom for themselves. Dissenters like Roger Williams were banished, and four Quakers were hanged.

**&ldquo;Christianity ended slavery.&rdquo;** and **&ldquo;Christianity justified slavery.&rdquo;**
Both statements are partly true, because both sides used the same book. Christian abolitionists played a major role in the movement, and Christian slaveholders and churches defended the system for generations. A fair account keeps both facts.

**&ldquo;Religion in America is disappearing.&rdquo;**
It is declining, but not disappearing. See the next section.

## Where things stand today

According to the Pew Research Center's 2023-24 Religious Landscape Study of nearly 37,000 adults, about 62 percent of American adults identify as Christian. Pew found that the long decline in Christian identification has slowed since around 2021, and that the share of people with no religion has also levelled off, at around three in ten. The same study found that younger Americans are much less likely to identify as Christian than older ones: 46 percent of the youngest group, against 80 percent of the oldest.

Other faiths, including Judaism, Islam, Buddhism and Hinduism, each make up a small share, mostly a few percent or less, but they have grown more visible. It is one of the most religiously diverse countries on Earth, and also one of the few rich countries where religion is still very important in public life.

## What I take from this

If I had to put the story in one sentence: the United States is a country that was founded by people escaping religious persecution, who sometimes became persecutors themselves, and who then wrote a law that made it harder for any religion to rule over the others. The result has not been perfect, as the list above shows. But as an experiment in keeping religion and government separate, it has mostly held, and that is why a Quaker, a Mormon, a Catholic, a Muslim and an atheist can all live in the same country today, with the same legal rights.
