India's own jet engine, the Kaveri, has just cleared a big test in Russia and is now being prepared for the country's stealth combat drone, the Ghatak. For many people, this sounds like sudden good news. In truth, it is the end of a very long and painful story that began in the 1980s, involved delays, missed targets and a lot of criticism, and is still not finished.

Let us go through it step by step: who started it, how it works, what went wrong, what went right, and what comes next.

## First, a common confusion: Russia did not start it

Many people think the Kaveri is connected to Russia because the news says "trials in Russia". That is not correct. The Kaveri is an **Indian design**, developed by the Gas Turbine Research Establishment (GTRE), a laboratory of the DRDO in Bengaluru.

Russia plays a different role. India does not have a facility that can test a jet engine at very high altitude and in real flight, so the engine is sent to Russian institutes for those tests. The high-altitude tests have been done at the Central Institute of Aviation Motors (CIAM), and the flight tests at the Gromov Flight Research Institute, where the engine is fitted on a modified Ilyushin Il-76 cargo aircraft used as a "flying testbed". Russia provides the test stage. The engine itself is India's.

## Why India wanted its own engine

A fighter jet is only as good as its engine. For decades India has had to buy engines from other countries, and supplies can be delayed or blocked by politics. After India's 1998 nuclear tests, for example, sanctions made some technology difficult to get. An engine built at home means independence.

In the 1980s, India began the Light Combat Aircraft program that later became the Tejas. The plan was to use an American GE F404 engine for the first aircraft, and to fit the Indian Kaveri on later production aircraft. The program was authorized in 1986, and full-scale development was approved in 1989. The original plan was to finish by 1996 at a cost of about ₹382 crore.

## How the engine is built, in simple words

A jet engine like the Kaveri works on one basic idea: suck air in, squeeze it, burn fuel in it, and blow the hot gas out the back very fast. The push backward gives the aircraft thrust.

The Kaveri has these main parts:

- **Fan and compressor:** a three-stage low-pressure fan and a six-stage high-pressure compressor squeeze the incoming air.
- **Combustor:** a ring-shaped chamber where fuel is mixed with the compressed air and burned.
- **Turbines:** one high-pressure and one low-pressure stage. They sit in the hot gas and spin the compressor and fan.
- **Afterburner (in the original version):** an extra section behind the turbine where more fuel is burned for a short, powerful burst of thrust.
- **Control computer (FADEC):** a digital system that controls fuel flow and keeps the engine safe. India has developed its own for the Kaveri.

The hardest part is the turbine. Its blades sit in gas hotter than the melting point of the metal itself, so they need special alloys, single-crystal casting and clever cooling. This is one of the big reasons jet engines are so hard to make. Only a handful of countries have mastered it.

## What is the "dry" Kaveri?

The new engine making the headlines is the **Kaveri Derivative Engine**, often called the **Kaveri Dry**. It is the same engine without an afterburner. A stealth drone like the Ghatak wants less heat and less noise, because an afterburner makes it easier for radars and infrared cameras to detect the aircraft. So a "dry" engine is actually better for that job.

| 1995-1996 | The core engine runs for the first time, and the first full prototype begins tests |

| 2004 | The engine fails its high-altitude tests in Russia, ending hopes of using it on the first production Tejas |

## Why did it fail for the Tejas?

The honest answer is that there was no single reason. There were many.

1. **Not enough thrust.** The original target was about 81 kN with afterburner. Reports say the engine reached around 70 to 75 kN, while the Tejas needed more.
2. **Too heavy.** An audit by the Comptroller and Auditor General around 2011 said the engine weighed about 1,235 kg against a design weight of 1,100 kg. More recently the weight has come down to about 1,180 kg.
3. **Technical problems.** Early on, the engine had problems such as turbine blades failing, and India had to buy blades and control systems from abroad.
4. **No test facilities at home.** Altitude testing had to be done in Russia, which meant long delays and high cost.
5. **Denied technology and sanctions.** Countries that have this technology are not keen to share it.
6. **Low funding and few specialists.** Former officials have said that India spent far less on engine development than countries like the US, Russia, France and China. For comparison, some Western engines cost well over a billion dollars each to develop.
7. **Unrealistic schedules and lack of openness.** The deadline moved from 1996 to 2000, then 2004, then 2009. Critics also said the DRDO was slow to admit problems and to seek foreign help.

By 2008 it was clear that the Tejas could not wait. India chose the American GE engines for it, and the Kaveri was delinked.

## How much did it cost?

The original plan was about ₹382 crore. By 2021, the government reported that ₹2,105 crore had been allocated and about ₹2,035 crore spent on the Kaveri program. Later, two projects for the dry engine were cleared at about ₹472 crore and ₹251 crore. That is a lot of money, but critics and supporters both point out that developing a modern combat engine from scratch typically costs far more in other countries.

## The good side

- **Real technology was built.** India now has its own digital engine control, single-crystal turbine blade casting, better alloys and forging methods.
- **Test experience.** Indian engineers have run an Indian-made engine in real flight conditions, which very few nations can say.
- **The dry engine beat its target.** The reported 48.5 kN thrust is above the roughly 46 kN goal, and trials also covered extreme cold conditions.
- **Industry is involved.** Companies such as Godrej Aerospace and Azad Engineering are building parts, which could grow India's private aerospace industry.
- **Spin-offs.** A marine gas turbine version of the Kaveri was developed for naval ships.

## The bad side

- **The original goal was missed.** The Kaveri never powered the Tejas fighter as planned.
- **Decades of delay.** It has been about 37 years since full development started.
- **Still not certified.** A trial success does not mean the engine is ready for service. It still needs airworthiness certification from CEMILAC, then integration with the Ghatak and flight trials with the Indian Air Force. One report estimates about two to three years before it is fully ready.
- **Still dependent on outside help.** The testing still happens in Russia, and any foreign partnership raises questions about how much technology will be shared.
- **Lower thrust class.** A 48.5 kN dry engine is suitable for a drone, but today's fighter jets, such as India's planned AMCA, need much bigger engines. India is reportedly looking at a joint effort with a foreign partner for that.

## What happens next?

The next steps are certification by CEMILAC in Bengaluru, fitting the engine into the Ghatak, and flight tests with the Indian Air Force. In parallel, work continues on a more powerful version, often called Kaveri 2.0, with an afterburner.

## A fair way to look at it

It is easy to call the Kaveri a failure, and as an engine for the Tejas it was. But building a jet engine is one of the hardest engineering tasks in the world, and only a few countries have succeeded. India's attempt did not produce the engine it wanted on time, but it produced people, factories, materials and experience. Whether that turns into a real, reliable, certified engine in the coming years is the question to watch.

I would be careful of both extremes: the claim that "India has finally built a world-class fighter engine" is too early, and the claim that "it was all a waste" ignores what was learned.
