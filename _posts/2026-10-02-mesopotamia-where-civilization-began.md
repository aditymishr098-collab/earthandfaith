Imagine a farmer, about 5,000 years ago, pressing a reed into wet clay to record how many sacks of barley he owed to the temple. He had no idea he was doing something that would change the world. But he was. That small mark was part of the first writing system in human history, and the land where it happened was called Mesopotamia.

Most people have heard the word. Fewer know how much of our daily life began there: cities, laws, schools, the 60-minute hour, even some of the stories we still tell. Let me take you through it.

## What does "Mesopotamia" even mean?

The name is Greek. It means "the land between the rivers", from *mesos* (middle) and *potamos* (river). The two rivers are the **Tigris** and the **Euphrates**, which run from the mountains of what is now Turkey down through Iraq and into the Persian Gulf. Parts of modern Syria and Iran belong to the same story.

It was part of a curved band of fertile land that people often call the **Fertile Crescent**. Farming had started around here roughly 10,000 years ago. But the south of Mesopotamia was a hard place to farm. It hardly rains there, and the rivers flood without warning, unlike the gentle, predictable Nile in Egypt. To survive, people had to dig canals, build embankments and share the work. That need to organize may be one reason why cities appeared here first.

## Sumer: the first cities

Around 4000 BCE, in the south, a people we call the **Sumerians** built cities such as **Uruk, Ur, Lagash and Eridu**. Uruk, in particular, grew into one of the biggest cities in the world of its time, with tens of thousands of people living behind its walls.

These were not countries in our sense. Each city was like a small state with its own ruler, its own temple and its own patron god. They traded, competed and fought, sometimes over water and borders.

## The invention of writing

This is the part I find most moving. Writing did not begin with poetry or prayers. It began as **accounting**.

Around 3400 to 3200 BCE in Uruk, people started pressing signs into clay tablets to keep track of grain, sheep, beer and labor. Over the next centuries the signs turned into a full writing system we call **cuneiform**, from the Latin word for "wedge", because the marks look like little wedges made by pressing a cut reed into clay.

Once people could write, they did not stop. Soon there were letters, contracts, school exercises, hymns, medical texts and stories. Clay lasts for thousands of years, and because of that, we have hundreds of thousands of Mesopotamian tablets today. We can read the actual complaints of people who lived 4,000 years ago, including one famous letter from a customer angry about the poor quality of copper.

## Poetry, and the first author we know by name

The first author in history whose name we know was a woman. **Enheduanna**, who lived around 2300 BCE, was the daughter of King Sargon of Akkad and served as high priestess of the moon god at Ur. She wrote hymns to the goddess Inanna, and she signed them in the first person. Her words are over 4,000 years old.

## Empires rise

Around 2334 BCE, a ruler named **Sargon of Akkad** conquered the Sumerian cities and created what is usually called the world's first empire. Others followed:

- **Ur III** (about 2100 BCE), when the great ziggurat of Ur was built.
- **Babylon**, under **Hammurabi** (ruled about 1792 to 1750 BCE).
- **Assyria**, a military power with its capitals at Ashur, Nimrud and Nineveh.
- **The Neo-Babylonian Empire**, under Nebuchadnezzar II (ruled 605 to 562 BCE).

Each empire borrowed from the last. The gods, the writing and many customs passed from Sumer to Babylon and Assyria, even when the language changed.

## Laws that still make us think

Mesopotamia gave us some of the earliest written laws. The oldest surviving code, that of **Ur-Nammu**, dates to around 2100 BCE. The most famous is the **Code of Hammurabi**, from about 1754 BCE. It was carved on a tall black stone and put on display so that people could see it. The stone is now in the Louvre in Paris.

It contains 282 laws about trade, marriage, theft, building and even doctors' mistakes. Many people remember it for "an eye for an eye". But it also shows something less pleasant: punishments often depended on who you were. Hurting someone of a higher class could cost much more than hurting a poorer person, which tells us a lot about the society behind the law.

## The gods of Mesopotamia

Religion shaped every part of life. People believed the world was run by many gods, and each city had its own special protector. The gods were thought to look like humans, with human feelings and needs.

| God or goddess | Linked to |
|---|---|
| Anu | The sky, the father of the gods |
| Enlil | Air and storms, a powerful and sometimes feared god |
| Enki (Ea) | Fresh water, wisdom and craft |
| Inanna (Ishtar) | Love, war and the morning and evening star |
| Utu (Shamash) | The sun and justice |
| Nanna (Sin) | The moon |
| Marduk | The patron god of Babylon, later the chief god |

Temples were not like churches or mosques where people gather to pray. They were considered the **houses of the gods**. Priests looked after the divine statue, dressing it, feeding it and honoring it each day. Beside many temples rose a **ziggurat**, a tall stepped tower of mud brick, which may have worked as a stairway between heaven and earth. The Great Ziggurat of Ur still stands in part today.

Mesopotamian religion had a rather different mood from what many people expect. In several of their stories, humans were created to **do the work the gods did not want to do**. There was no promise of a happy heaven for ordinary people. The dead went to a gloomy underworld, sometimes called the "House of Dust", where they lived a shadowy existence. This made the life on Earth feel very important.

People also believed that the gods sent messages through omens: the position of the stars, the shape of a sheep's liver, unusual events. Priests became careful observers of the sky, and that habit helped begin early astronomy.

## The Epic of Gilgamesh

The most famous story of the ancient world is the **Epic of Gilgamesh**, about a king of Uruk who becomes a friend of a wild man named Enkidu. When Enkidu dies, Gilgamesh is terrified of death and goes on a long search for eternal life.

Along the way, he meets a man who survived a great flood sent by the gods, and who tells him how he saved his family by building a boat. If you know the story of Noah, you may notice the similarity. Many scholars think that these flood stories are connected, and that the people of the ancient Near East shared and reshaped common tales. The Mesopotamian version is older than the written Biblical one.

At the end, Gilgamesh fails to win immortality, but he returns to Uruk and looks at its walls with new eyes. The message is gentle and human: we cannot live forever, but what we build can outlive us.

In 1872 a British scholar named George Smith was reading a tablet from Nineveh when he realized he had found the flood story. He is said to have been so excited that he began to undress. True or not, it shows what a shock it was to find that the Bible was not the only ancient flood story.

## Science, maths and everyday inventions

Mesopotamians were practical and curious. Here are some things that started or developed there:

- **The base-60 number system.** We still use it when we divide an hour into 60 minutes, a minute into 60 seconds and a circle into 360 degrees.
- **Advanced mathematics.** A tablet called Plimpton 322, from around 1800 BCE, lists number triples that match the Pythagorean theorem, more than a thousand years before Pythagoras.
- **Astronomy and calendars.** They tracked the Moon and the planets for centuries and kept detailed records.
- **The wheel.** Some of the earliest evidence of wheeled vehicles comes from this region, around 3500 BCE.
- **Irrigation and canals,** which allowed farming in dry land.
- **Schools.** The *edubba*, or "tablet house", trained scribes, and students copied texts again and again.
- **Beer.** Barley beer was a staple, and one of the oldest songs we have is a hymn to Ninkasi, the goddess of beer. Workers were sometimes paid in beer rations.

## The famous city of Babylon

Babylon was already old when Hammurabi ruled it, but it reached its peak under **Nebuchadnezzar II**, who rebuilt it with massive walls and a beautiful gate decorated with blue glazed bricks, the **Ishtar Gate**. A rebuilt version of the gate is now in a museum in Berlin.

You may have heard of the **Hanging Gardens of Babylon**, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. The truth is that nobody has ever found proof of them in Babylon. Some scholars think they might have been in Nineveh, and others suspect they were mostly a legend. It is a good reminder that famous ancient stories are not always backed by evidence.

## Assyria and the first great library

The Assyrians were famous as fierce warriors, and their kings left carvings that boasted about their conquests. But one Assyrian king, **Ashurbanipal** (ruled about 668 to 631 BCE), also loved books. In his capital, Nineveh, he built a library holding tens of thousands of clay tablets, which included the Epic of Gilgamesh. When the city was destroyed in 612 BCE, the tablets were buried in the ruins. That, ironically, saved them. Archaeologists found them in the 19th century, and they are now mostly in the British Museum.

## How it all ended

Over time, Mesopotamia lost its independence:

- In **539 BCE**, the Persian king Cyrus conquered Babylon.
- In **331 BCE**, Alexander the Great took the region.
- Later, Persians, Romans and others ruled in turn.

Cuneiform slowly faded away. The latest known cuneiform tablet, an astronomical text, dates to around the first century CE, so the script was used for about 3,000 years. After that, the knowledge to read it was lost until scholars cracked it in the 19th century.

Some researchers also argue that farming in the south may have been weakened by salt building up in the soil because of irrigation. This is debated, and it was probably one cause among many.

## Why Mesopotamia still matters

When I think about Mesopotamia, I think about how human it all feels. People worried about taxes, argued over land, wrote love poems, complained about bad service and feared death. They looked at the sky and asked what it all meant. That is exactly what we still do.

The history of Mesopotamia is also under threat. The Iraq Museum in Baghdad was looted in 2003, and in 2015 the Islamic State destroyed parts of ancient sites such as Nimrud. Protecting these places matters, because once a ruin is gone, we cannot get its stories back.
