Right now, as you read this, two machines built in the 1970s are still working. They are further from Earth than anything humans have ever made, they run on computers weaker than a modern car key, and they are still sending messages home. They are called Voyager 1 and Voyager 2, and their story is one of the most beautiful things people have ever done.

Let me tell it from the beginning.

## A rare chance that comes once in 175 years

In the 1960s, a young engineer named Gary Flandro noticed something at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory. In the late 1970s, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune would line up in a way that happens only about once every 175 years. A spacecraft launched at the right time could use the gravity of each planet to swing onward to the next, like a skater being flung from hand to hand. It would save decades of travel.

NASA called this idea the "Grand Tour". The original plan was too expensive and got cut back, but a smaller version survived. Two spacecraft would be built, and they would aim for Jupiter and Saturn, with a chance to continue further if all went well. The official mission was meant to last about five years.

## Launch, and a funny detail about the names

Voyager 2 launched first, on August 20, 1977. Voyager 1 followed on September 5, 1977. Yes, the one called "1" left second. It was simply faster, and it would reach Jupiter and Saturn before its twin.

Both left from Cape Canaveral in Florida on a Titan-Centaur rocket. Each spacecraft was about the size of a small car and weighed around 800 kilograms, with a big dish antenna about 3.7 meters wide.

## What they saw

**Jupiter (1979).** Voyager 1 arrived in March and Voyager 2 in July. The photographs were nothing like what telescopes had shown. The Great Red Spot turned out to be a giant storm. Jupiter had a faint ring that nobody knew about. And on one of its moons, Io, a young scientist named Linda Morabito spotted something strange in a picture: a plume rising from the edge of the moon. It was a volcano erupting. It was the first time anyone had seen an active volcano outside Earth.

**Saturn (1980 and 1981).** The rings were far more complicated than anyone imagined, made of thousands of thin ringlets. Voyager 1 also passed Titan, a moon with a thick orange atmosphere, and learned how dense and mysterious it was. That close pass bent Voyager 1's path upward, out of the plane where the planets travel. Its planet-visiting days were over.

**Uranus (1986) and Neptune (1989).** Only Voyager 2 continued, and it is still the only spacecraft that has ever visited these two planets. At Uranus it found tilted magnetic fields and a strange, cracked moon called Miranda. At Neptune it found a dark storm, fast winds and geysers of nitrogen on its moon Triton.

If you think about it, nearly everything we know up close about Uranus and Neptune comes from a few days of flying by in the 1980s.

## A photo of a dot

In 1990, Voyager 1 was about six billion kilometers from home. The astronomer Carl Sagan asked NASA to turn the camera around and take one last look back. Some people worried it could damage the camera by pointing near the Sun, but the team agreed.

The picture shows Earth as a tiny pale dot, smaller than a pixel, caught in a faint beam of sunlight. It is known as the **Pale Blue Dot**. Sagan later wrote about it, and the message was simple: everyone you have ever known, every war, every prayer, every king and farmer, lived on that speck.

I think about that photograph often. It does not tell us what to believe, but it does remind us how small our arguments look from far away.

## A message in a bottle

Both spacecraft carry a gold-plated copper record called the **Golden Record**. It is a message for anyone, or anything, that might find the probes in the far future.

A team led by Carl Sagan chose what went on it:

- **Greetings in 55 languages**, including Hindi and other Indian languages, as well as the sound of a whale song
- **115 images** of life on Earth, from a mother nursing her baby to a supermarket to the structure of DNA
- **Sounds of Earth**: thunder, birds, a kiss, a mother and child, a footstep, a train
- **About 90 minutes of music** from around the world, from Bach and Beethoven to Chuck Berry, and, from India, a raga sung by Kesarbai Kerkar called *Jaat Kahan Ho*

The records come with instructions drawn on the cover, showing how to play them. The odds that anyone will ever find it are tiny. But it was never really only for aliens. It was a way for us to say what we are proud of.

## Leaving the Sun's bubble

The Sun blows out a constant stream of particles called the solar wind. It creates a huge bubble around the whole solar system, called the heliosphere. For years, scientists asked: where does it end?

Voyager 1 crossed the edge in August 2012 and entered interstellar space, the first human-made object to do so. Voyager 2 followed in November 2018, and since it had a working plasma instrument, it measured the crossing in much more detail. What they found was surprising: the edge of the Sun's bubble was more complex than scientists had expected.

## How are they still alive?

The Voyagers do not use solar panels, because the Sun is far too faint out there. They run on **radioisotope generators**, which turn the heat of slowly decaying plutonium into electricity. The catch is that the power drops every year, by about four watts or so. That means the engineers must keep switching things off.

The numbers are almost unbelievable:

| What | Voyager's version |
|---|---|
| Computer memory | About 69 kilobytes, far less than a single photo on your phone |
| Transmitter power | About 23 watts, like a fridge light |
| Speed (Voyager 1) | Roughly 17 kilometers per second |
| Signal travel time | Nearly a full day each way |

Over the years, instruments have been turned off one by one. Voyager 1 has already lost most of its instruments, and now just two are left, one that listens to plasma waves and one that measures magnetic fields. In April 2026, NASA turned off one more, the Low-energy Charged Particles experiment, to keep the spacecraft going. Voyager 2 also keeps only a few instruments, and the number keeps shrinking. The engineers decided the order of shutdowns years ago, together with the scientists.

The team is also testing a new way to save power, which they nicknamed the "Big Bang". It is risky, because if something goes wrong the spacecraft might need a long recovery. I do not know how those tests turned out, so for the latest, please check NASA's Voyager page.

## A few scary days, and some clever fixes

The Voyagers have had some very close calls.

In **2023**, a command sent by mistake pointed Voyager 2's antenna slightly away from Earth, and it went silent. The team used a powerful transmitter at the Deep Space Network to "shout" at it, and it responded.

In **November 2023**, Voyager 1 started sending gibberish instead of real data. After months of detective work, engineers found that a tiny memory chip had failed. They rewrote the code, splitting it up and storing it in different places, and in 2024 Voyager 1 began making sense again. This was done by sending instructions that take about a day to arrive, to a computer that is almost 50 years old.

## The big milestone coming soon

In **November 2026**, Voyager 1 will become the first spacecraft ever to reach one light-day from Earth. That is the distance light travels in 24 hours, roughly 25 billion kilometers. It means that if you send a message to Voyager 1, it will take a full day to arrive, and another day for the reply.

Voyager 2 is expected to reach the same distance around November 2035, though no one knows whether it will still be working then.

Both probes will mark their 50th anniversary in 2027. They may keep at least one instrument running into the 2030s, though this is a hope and not a promise.

## Where are they going?

They do not have a destination. They are just flying. In roughly 40,000 years, Voyager 1 will pass within about 1.6 light-years of a faint star called AC+79 3888, and Voyager 2 will pass within a similar distance of Ross 248. Both will keep wandering around our galaxy, long after the Sun, the Earth and every human language we know has changed beyond recognition.

## Why I love this story

The Voyagers were built by people who knew they would not live to see the end. Many of the engineers who launched them have since retired, and some have died. Yet the machines keep going, carrying a record of what we sounded like, what we looked like and what we hoped for.

They were meant to work for five years. They have worked for nearly fifty. It is one of the best examples I know of what happens when curiosity, patience and careful thinking come together.
