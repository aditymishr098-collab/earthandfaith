Most of the things we fear announce themselves. A fire is loud, a flood is visible, a snake hisses. Asbestos does none of that. It is a soft, quiet mineral that was mixed into roofs, brakes, school ceilings and pipes for more than a century, and the people it hurt usually did not find out for thirty or forty years.

This post follows it from the rock to the lung: where asbestos comes from, how it was turned into products, what it does to the body, and how many people it kills. I give numbers where I can and I say where they are rough. I also look at the claims people still make in its defence, because some of them are still being made today.

## What asbestos actually is

Asbestos is not one mineral. It is a name for six naturally occurring minerals that all share one habit: they split into extremely thin, strong fibres. They fall into two families.

- **Serpentine family:** only one member, **chrysotile**, or white asbestos. Its fibres are curly. It is by far the most widely used type.
- **Amphibole family:** **amosite** (brown), **crocidolite** (blue), **tremolite**, **actinolite** and **anthophyllite**. Their fibres are straight and needle-like.

The name comes from Greek. It is usually translated as "unquenchable" or "inextinguishable", because the mineral does not burn. A related Greek word, *amiantos*, meant "undefiled", which was used for cloth that came out of a fire clean. That fire resistance is the whole reason the world fell in love with it.

## Where it comes from

Asbestos minerals form in certain rocks when hot, mineral-rich fluids change the rock deep underground. Chrysotile comes from serpentinite rock, which is found in many parts of the world. When the rock is later pushed up and exposed, the fibre veins can be dug out.

People noticed these fibres a very long time ago. Archaeologists found that people in what is now Finland mixed asbestos into their pottery about 4,500 years ago to make it stronger. The Greeks and Romans wove it into cloth and lamp wicks. The Roman writer Pliny the Elder described it, and his writings helped spread the word.

Large-scale mining is much younger. It really began in the 1870s and 1880s:

- **Canada:** Asbestos from Quebec was displayed in London in 1862. By the 1870s the Thetford Hills were being mined, and output grew from about 50 tons in 1878 to over 10,000 tons by the 1890s. The Jeffrey Mine in Quebec became the largest asbestos mine in the world for decades.
- **Russia:** Mining in the Ural mountains started in the 1880s.
- **South Africa:** Large mines opened from 1893.
- **United States, Italy and others** followed.

## How it was made into products

Making raw asbestos useful is not complicated, and that was part of its appeal. The rock is dug out, crushed and milled so that the fibres separate from the stone. The fibres are then graded by length, because long fibres can be spun into cloth and short ones are used as filler.

After that, the fibres go into whatever needed to be strong, cheap and fireproof:

- **Asbestos cement:** fibres mixed with Portland cement and water, then pressed into corrugated roofing sheets, flat panels and pipes. This is still the biggest use of asbestos in the world today.
- **Insulation:** wrapped around boilers, steam pipes and ship engines, sprayed on ceilings and steel beams.
- **Friction products:** brake pads and clutch plates.
- **Others:** floor tiles, textiles, fire blankets, gaskets, and even some talc and vermiculite products that were contaminated with it.

During the Second World War, about 4.3 million American workers are estimated to have been exposed to asbestos in shipbuilding alone. It was in every ship, because a fire at sea is a disaster.

## How we found out it was killing people

The warning signs came remarkably early, and for decades they were ignored or hidden.

| Year | What happened |
|---|---|
| 1898 | British factory inspectors raise concerns about asbestos dust |
| 1927 | The lung disease gets its name: asbestosis |
| 1938 | Animal studies confirm toxicity, and "safe" dust limits are set (which later turn out to be far too high) |
| 1960 | Mesothelioma, a fatal cancer, is firmly linked to asbestos |
| 1982 to 2003 | Sweden, Iceland, the UK, Finland and Australia ban or phase out most uses |
| 2012 | Japan bans it. Canada stops mining that year as well |
| 2018 | Canada bans asbestos in all its forms |
| 2024 | The United States fully bans imports of raw asbestos fibre |

Two places show how it played out on the ground.

**Wittenoom, Australia.** A crocidolite (blue asbestos) mine operated from 1943 to 1966. Dust in the mine and mill was often measured at six to eight times the supposedly safe level. The toll had reached 500 deaths by 1989, and predictions at the time were for around 2,000 in the end. The Australian government banned all asbestos on 31 December 2003.

**Libby, Montana.** A mine near this small American town produced vermiculite, a product used in insulation and gardening, which was contaminated with a form of asbestos called tremolite. It opened around 1919, W.R. Grace took control in 1963, and the mine closed in 1990. Some estimates say about 400 residents died from the dust, and thousands more have been made ill, including people who never worked in the mine. The US government has spent over 600 million dollars on cleanup, and the town was named a Superfund site.

## How asbestos harms the body

The danger comes from breathing the fibres in. Fibres are so thin and light that they stay in the air for a long time, and once they are in the lung they cannot be dissolved or coughed out easily. They stay. Over years, the body keeps trying to deal with them, and that is what causes the damage.

The main diseases are:

- **Asbestosis:** scarring of the lungs. It makes breathing harder and harder, and it cannot be cured.
- **Lung cancer:** the risk rises with the amount of exposure. Smoking makes it much worse. The two risks do not simply add up, they multiply.
- **Mesothelioma:** a cancer of the thin lining around the lungs or abdomen. It is rare in people who were never exposed to asbestos, which is why it is called the signature cancer of asbestos. It is usually found late and is often fatal within a year or two of diagnosis.
- **Other cancers:** the World Health Organization lists cancers of the larynx and the ovary as well.

Two things make asbestos especially cruel. First, **the delay**. Disease usually appears 20 to 40 years or more after exposure, so a person who breathed the dust at 25 may get sick at 65. By then the factory may be closed and the employer gone. Second, **it reaches people who never touched it**. Wives who washed their husbands' dusty work clothes, children in homes near mines, and whole towns like Libby have been affected.

All six types are linked to cancer. The WHO says that all forms of asbestos, including chrysotile, cause cancer in humans, and that no safe level of exposure has been identified.

## The death rate

This is the hardest number to pin down, because the deaths are spread over decades and many are recorded as plain lung cancer.

- **WHO:** more than 200,000 deaths a year are estimated to be caused by occupational exposure to asbestos. That is more than 70 percent of all deaths from work-related cancer.
- **In daily terms:** 200,000 a year works out to roughly 550 deaths every day.
- **Other estimates are higher.** Counts that also include people exposed outside work, such as family members and residents near sources, put the yearly total higher still. One commonly cited figure is about 255,000, and a 2017 study suggested the total might pass 300,000.
- **Earlier estimates were much lower:** around 100,000 a year in 2006 and 107,000 occupational deaths in 2004. This does not mean asbestos got twice as deadly. It means we got better at counting.

One honest caution: because of the long delay, today's death numbers reflect exposure from decades ago. In countries that banned asbestos early, the numbers should eventually fall. In countries still using it heavily, the deaths of tomorrow are being made now.

## Who still mines and uses it

According to the US Geological Survey, world mine production of asbestos was about 949,000 metric tons in 2024, with an estimate of 960,000 tons for 2025.

| Country | 2024 production (tons) |
|---|---|
| Russia | 306,900 |
| China | 250,000 |
| Kazakhstan | 225,700 |
| Brazil | 166,890 |

Brazil appears on the list even though it banned use of asbestos at home in 2017, so a part of its output is for export. The demand that remains is mostly for cement roofing and pipes in Asia.

The United States closed its last asbestos mine in 2002 and fully banned imports of raw fibre in May 2024. It is not a total ban yet: chlorine plants and some other uses are being phased out over the coming years. The WHO says the use of asbestos has been prohibited in more than 50 of its member states, and other counts of countries and territories with full bans go higher.

## What about India?

This part matters to many of my readers, so I want to be careful with it.

India banned asbestos mining in 1993, but it did not ban the use of asbestos. It has instead become one of the world's biggest consumers. According to the International Ban Asbestos Secretariat, India imported about 436,000 tons in 2021 and about 403,000 tons in 2022, which makes it the largest importer in the world. The fibre comes from Russia, Brazil, Kazakhstan and other countries, and most of it goes into cement roofing sheets and pipes.

There has been a small step: in April 2025 the Education Ministry banned the use of asbestos in the construction and refurbishment of Kendriya Vidyalayas and Jawahar Navodaya Vidyalayas. That covers schools, not the rest of the country, and import and use for other purposes continue.

## Claims and what the evidence says

**"White asbestos (chrysotile) is safe if it is used carefully."**
This is the claim the industry has made for decades, and the argument is about "controlled use". The WHO's position is clear: all types, including chrysotile, cause cancer, and no safe threshold has been found. In practice, controlled use is hard to keep up in small factories, on construction sites, and during demolition and repair years later.

**"Asbestos roofing is only dangerous if you break it."**
Partly true, and worth understanding. Intact cement sheets release very little. The risk grows when sheets are cut, drilled, sanded, weathered or broken, and when they are removed. The people at greatest risk are the workers who make them, install them and tear them down.

**"It is natural, so it cannot be that harmful."**
Natural does not mean safe. Arsenic and lead are natural too. What matters is how a substance behaves in the body, and asbestos fibres stay in the lung for life.

**"Only workers get sick."**
Mostly workers, but not only. Family members who handled dusty clothes and whole communities such as Libby, Montana were affected.

**"Banning it costs jobs and makes housing more expensive."**
This is a real argument, and it is not silly. Asbestos cement is cheap, and many poorer regions depend on cheap roofing. But substitutes made from organic fibres, cement and silica have been in use for decades, and countries that moved to them, such as Australia and Japan, managed it. A fair question is who pays for the change and who pays for the disease, and the second bill is usually bigger and arrives later.

## If you think you have asbestos at home

- You cannot tell by looking. Only a laboratory test of a small sample can tell you, and it should be taken by a trained person.
- If the material is in good condition and not being disturbed, the usual advice is to leave it alone and keep an eye on it.
- Do not drill, scrub, sand, cut or break it, and do not sweep or vacuum the dust.
- If it must be removed, use trained, licensed professionals who wet the material and seal the waste. This is not a weekend job.

## What I take from this

Asbestos was not stupidity. It was a real wonder: strong, cheap, fireproof and everywhere. That is exactly why it is such a good lesson. The warnings arrived in 1898, the disease was named in 1927, the cancer was confirmed in 1960, and the world is still making and using it in 2026. The dead of tomorrow are being decided today, by people who will not feel anything for another thirty years.

A mineral does not care what we believe about it. That is the point of looking at the evidence, and it applies to dust in a roof just as it applies to every other claim on this blog.
