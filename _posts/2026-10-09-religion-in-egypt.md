Egypt is a special case, because almost no other country has had so many religions on the same land for so long. Pharaonic religion lasted about three thousand years. Christianity then became the main faith for centuries, and Islam has been the majority faith for many centuries since. All of them left their marks, and all of them are still visible if you walk through Cairo.

I have already written about [the gods and the afterlife of ancient Egypt]({% post_url 2026-09-29-ancient-egypt-land-of-gods-and-afterlife %}), so I will keep that part short and spend most of this post on what happened after it.

## The age of the old gods

For thousands of years, Egyptians worshipped many gods tied to the sun, the Nile, the sky and the dead: Ra, Osiris, Isis, Horus, Anubis and many more. Temples were powerful institutions with land and priests, and the pharaoh was seen as the link between gods and people. Belief in the afterlife shaped almost everything, from tombs to mummification.

There was one famous experiment. The pharaoh Akhenaten, who ruled around 1353 to 1336 BCE, pushed worship of the sun disc, the Aten, above all other gods and built a new capital for it. Whether this counts as true monotheism is debated: many Egyptologists call it something closer to worshipping one god above the others. What is clear is that it did not last. His successor, Tutankhamun, moved back to the old gods, and later rulers removed Akhenaten's name from the king lists. Some writers link Akhenaten to Moses, but there is no evidence for it.

## Christianity arrives

By the time Egypt was under Greek and then Roman rule, Alexandria was one of the greatest cities in the world, full of Greek, Egyptian and Jewish communities. Christian tradition says Saint Mark brought the faith there around 42 CE. The earliest solid evidence is a fragment of the Gospel of John in the Coptic language from the 2nd century, and some historians think Christians may have become the majority by the early 3rd century.

It was not an easy road. Under the emperor Diocletian, from 284, Christians were killed in large numbers. That period was so important to Egyptian Christians that they still count their years from it: the Coptic calendar begins in 284 and its years are marked "A.M.", for Anno Martyrum, the Year of the Martyrs.

Then the tables turned. Once Christianity became the favoured religion of the empire, the old temples came under attack. In 391, Bishop Theophilus had the Serapeum of Alexandria, one of the city's great pagan temples, demolished.

## Hypatia, 415

The most famous victim of those tensions was Hypatia, a philosopher and teacher of mathematics and astronomy in Alexandria. In March 415, during Lent, a Christian mob led by a man named Peter, a lector, pulled her from her carriage, took her into a church, and killed her with broken pottery. The historian Socrates Scholasticus says it was political jealousy: Hypatia was close to the city's Roman prefect, Orestes, who was feuding with the bishop, Cyril, and rumour said she stood in the way of a reconciliation between them.

Whether Cyril ordered it is debated, and there is no firm evidence that he did. But the atmosphere is documented: the year before, Cyril had closed the city's synagogues and expelled many of its Jews, though how many is disputed. One detail that people often leave out: Orestes was also a Christian. This was a fight over power as well as belief.

## A church that split

In 451, the Council of Chalcedon argued over the nature of Christ. The Egyptian delegation rejected its decisions and the Egyptian patriarch was deposed. Egypt's church split in two: one side accepted Chalcedon and became the Greek Orthodox Church of Alexandria, and the other became the ancestor of the Coptic Orthodox Church. The word "Copt" itself comes from the Greek word for Egyptian, and the Coptic language is the last stage of ancient Egyptian, written in Greek letters.

## The Arab conquest and the slow change

Between 639 and about 646, Arab armies under Amr ibn al-As took Egypt from the Byzantine Empire. At first, many Egyptians did not mind a change of ruler, since they had little love for Byzantine rule and its religious pressure. Christians and Jews were allowed to keep their faiths as "People of the Book", but they paid a special tax called the jizya. Over time, pressure grew: heavy taxes and forced labour, and under some rulers the jizya was extended even to monks. The Copts rose in revolt several times, including the Bashmurian revolts from about 720 to 832.

This is the part that gets simplified most often, so I want to be clear. Egypt did not become Muslim in a day, and it was not converted at the point of a sword. The conquest was military, but the change of religion took centuries and was driven by taxes, social advantage and sometimes fear. Historians disagree about when Muslims became the majority. Estimates run from around the 9th century to well after the 14th.

## Caliphs, Crusaders and sultans

The record under different rulers is very uneven.

- **Al-Hakim (Fatimid caliph, ruled 996 to 1021)** is the harshest example. In 1004 he banned Christians from celebrating Epiphany and Easter. In 1005 Christians and Jews were forced to wear distinctive clothing: Christians had to wear a heavy iron cross. In 1009 he ordered the destruction of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem. Then, from about 1012, he reversed course and allowed forced converts to return to their faiths. Historians still argue about his motives. A new religion, the Druze faith, grew out of beliefs about him.
- **Saladin (1171)** ended Fatimid rule and returned Egypt to Sunni Islam.
- **The Crusades** also reached Egypt. Crusader armies attacked the port of Damietta in the Fifth Crusade (1218 to 1221) and again in the Seventh (1249 to 1250).
- **The Ottomans** took Egypt in 1517 and ruled it for centuries.

## The modern era: minorities shrink

In the 20th century, the story changed again.

**Egypt's Jews.** Before the end of the monarchy in 1953, about 80,000 Jews lived in Egypt, and the 1948 census counted 65,639. After the creation of Israel, wars and the 1956 Suez Crisis, which led to mass expulsions, almost all left. By 2021 fewer than 20 remained.

**Bahá'ís.** The faith was legally recognised in 1925, but a 1960 law banned Bahá'í institutions and activities. Estimates of the community range from about 1,000 to several thousand.

**Islam in the law.** Article 2 of the constitution names Islam as the state religion and Islamic law as the main source of legislation, a wording that was strengthened in 1980. Only Islam, Christianity and Judaism are formally recognised.

## What religion has cost in lives in Egypt

Violence against Copts and other minorities is a real and repeated pattern. Here is a list of the major attacks I could find. The numbers vary by source, and it is not complete.

| Event | Year | Deaths (approx.) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypatia murdered in Alexandria | 415 | 1 | Religious and political conflict |
| El-Kosheh riots | 1999 to 2000 | 21 | 20 Christians and 1 Muslim |
| Church bombing, Alexandria | 2011 | 21 | New Year's Day |
| Maspero demonstrations, Cairo | 2011 | 24 to 27 | Mostly Coptic protesters; army blamed in reports |
| Church bombing, Cairo | 2016 | about 29 | Suicide bombing |
| Palm Sunday bombings, Tanta and Alexandria | 2017 | 45 or more | Two churches |
| Bus attack, Minya | 2017 | 28 | Christian pilgrims |
| Bus attack, Minya | 2018 | 7 | Christian pilgrims |
| Egyptian Copts killed in Libya | 2015 | 20 | Killed by the Islamic State |
| Al-Rawda mosque attack, Sinai | 2017 | 311 | Sufi Muslims, attacked by militants linked to the Islamic State |

I added the last line deliberately. Extremist violence in Egypt has not only targeted Christians. The Sinai mosque attack of November 2017, which killed 311 people including 27 children, was aimed at Sufi Muslims whom the militants considered heretics. Muslims have been victims of religious extremism in Egypt too.

In 2013, after the removal of President Morsi, churches were attacked across the country: at least 45, by one count. And in 2011 a human chain of Muslims formed outside churches in Alexandria during Christmas Mass, a week after the bombing, to protect the worshippers. Both of those things are true, and both belong in the picture.

## Claims and what the evidence says

**"The pyramids were built by slaves."**
This idea goes back to the Greeks, writing long after the pyramids were built. Archaeologists today believe the Great Pyramid was built by tens of thousands of skilled workers, some paid and some serving as tax labour in rotation, who were fed with bread and beer. Their camps and their cemeteries have been found near the site, and the tombs there include inscriptions describing how the workforce was organised. For another pyramid, from the Middle Kingdom, there is evidence of workers from Canaan. So "slaves built the pyramids" has no good support, and neither does "no foreigners ever worked there".

**"There is archaeological proof of the Exodus."**
The mainstream view among archaeologists is that the Exodus as the Torah tells it is not historical. A century of work has found no direct evidence of the captivity, the escape or the journey, though small groups of Semitic-speaking people did move in and out of Egypt in that period. Some scholars think a small group with a real memory of Egypt may stand behind the story. That is a long way from proof of the story as told. You can respect the story and still be honest about what the digs have and have not found.

**"Akhenaten invented monotheism."**
He is the first well-known ruler to push one god above the rest, but scholars disagree on whether it was monotheism, and it did not outlive him.

**"Egypt was always Muslim," or "Egypt was converted by force."**
Neither. Egypt was Christian in majority for centuries after the conquest and became Muslim slowly. Pressure and force played a part, and so did ordinary things like taxes and social advantage.

**"Copts are the real Egyptians."**
The word Copt does mean Egyptian, and the Coptic language comes from ancient Egyptian. But religion does not tell you ancestry. Egyptians of both faiths are Egyptians, and many Muslim Egyptians descend from Christians who converted over the centuries.

**"Hypatia was killed because Christianity hates science."**
The truth is messier. She was killed by a Christian mob in a conflict over political power between two officials, one of them a fellow Christian. The case does show how mobs, religion and politics can combine into violence. It is not a clean story of faith versus science.

## Where things stand today

Egypt has over 100 million people. Estimates of the Muslim share run from 85 to 95 percent, mostly Sunni. Estimates for Christians, who are mainly Copts, vary from about 5 to 15 percent: the Pew Research Center put it at 5.1 percent in 2010, the CIA World Factbook says about 10 percent, the Arab Barometer found 9.6 percent in 2018, and in 2023 Pope Tawadros II spoke of about 15 million Copts, roughly 14 percent. Nobody has an exact figure, because religion has not been published in the census since 2006. Egypt also has the largest Christian community in the Middle East.

Some other facts on freedom of belief:

- The constitution guarantees freedom of belief, but the practice is limited by law.
- **Blasphemy law:** public statements seen as insulting Islam or Christianity can be prosecuted, but insulting other religions cannot. Atheists such as Alber Saber and Kareem Amer have been convicted.
- **ID cards** list religion, and for years only the three recognised faiths could be written there. Court rulings in 2008 allowed members of other faiths to get papers by leaving the field blank.
- **Church building:** a 2016 law made it easier to get approval for new churches, and thousands have since been legalised. Reports say security services still block some.

## What I take from this

Egypt shows how religion layers rather than disappears. Mosques, churches and the ruins of ancient temples stand within a few kilometres of each other. Each new faith arrived with power in its hands, and each new ruler decided how to treat those who believed differently. The record is a mix: tolerance and cruelty, protection and persecution, sometimes under the same ruler in the same decade.

What stands out for me is how often the trouble was less about what people believed and more about who held power and who paid the tax. That is a lesson for any country in this series.
