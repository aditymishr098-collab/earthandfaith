Look at your hand for a moment. It can write a letter, hold a baby, throw a ball or play a song. Behind your eyes, a brain is asking a strange question: what am I? Of all the animals on Earth, we are the only one that wonders about its own existence. That alone makes us worth a closer look.

## A very young species

Modern humans, *Homo sapiens*, are surprisingly new. The oldest fossils that look like us were found in Morocco and are about 300,000 years old. Earth itself is about 4.5 billion years old, so if its whole history were a single day, we would show up in the last few seconds before midnight.

Our family tree is also more crowded than people imagine. For much of our history, we shared the planet with other human species, such as the Neanderthals. Modern DNA studies show that many people alive today carry a small amount of Neanderthal DNA. We were not just neighbors, sometimes we were family.

## Closer to animals than we like to think

Genetically, we are very close to chimpanzees. Depending on how it is measured, our DNA is around 98 to 99 percent similar. That does not mean we are "98 percent chimp", because small differences in the right places can make a huge difference. But it does remind us that we are not separate from nature. We are a branch of it.

## What makes us different

Still, something unusual happened in our story. Three things stand out.

- **Language.** We can describe things that are not in front of us, like the past, the future and imaginary worlds. This lets us share knowledge across generations.
- **Cooperation at a large scale.** Ants and bees cooperate too, but humans can work together with millions of strangers, because we share ideas such as money, laws and beliefs.
- **Shared stories.** Myths, religions, nations and science all began as things people imagined together and then built into reality. Some stories comfort us, some explain the world, and some, like science, test themselves against evidence.

Our brain is at the center of it all. It weighs only about 1.3 kg, yet it holds around 86 billion nerve cells. Nobody fully understands how it produces thoughts, feelings and the sense of "I".

## Small bodies, big questions

Physically, humans are not impressive. We are slower than many animals, weaker than many, and our babies are helpless for years. Our strength has always been each other. We care for our young, teach our children and look after the sick, and this habit of caring may be one of the oldest things that made us human.

## So, what does it mean?

There is no single answer. To a biologist, being human is about a body and a genome. To a philosopher, it is about consciousness and choice. To a religious person, it may be about a soul. To all of us, it is about the wish to understand and to belong.

I do not think we need to settle the answer to enjoy the question. The fact that a bunch of atoms on a small planet can ask "who are we?" is itself one of the most remarkable things in the universe.
