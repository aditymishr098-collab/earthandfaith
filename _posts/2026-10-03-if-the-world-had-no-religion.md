I want to start with an honest confession: nobody can give you the real number. Anyone who tells you "religion has killed exactly X million people" is either guessing or selling something. History does not come with a spreadsheet, and the question "what if there had been no religion?" asks us to rebuild a whole alternate world. We cannot do that.

But we can do something useful. We can go through the major incidents one by one, look at the best estimates of how many people died, ask how much religion really had to do with each one, and then see what is left. That is what this article tries to do. I will also tell you where the numbers are weak, because pretending to be sure would be the same sin I am criticising.

One rule for this whole post, and for this whole website: I am criticising ideas, not people. Most believers in every religion are peaceful people who want to feed their children and live quietly. This is about what happens when certain ideas are given power, and about what we can learn from that.

## First, a reality check on "religion causes wars"

There is a popular claim that religion is behind most wars. The data does not support it. The *Encyclopedia of Wars* by Charles Phillips and Alan Axelrod lists 1,763 conflicts in recorded history, and only about 121 of them, roughly 7 percent, are classed as religious wars. Even that figure has problems. It counts conflicts, not deaths, and the classification is a judgement call. A war between two kingdoms where the king also happens to use religious language might be counted either way.

So the honest starting point is this: religion is not the main cause of war. Land, power, money, revenge, and fear of the neighbour cause most of it. But religion is a very effective *amplifier*. It can turn "they took our field" into "they are the enemy of God," and once people believe that, ordinary limits disappear. That is the part worth studying.

## The big wars where religion was central or very close to it

### The Crusades (1095 to about 1291)

A series of campaigns, mostly launched by the Latin Church, to take Jerusalem and the Holy Land. They also included massacres of Jewish communities in the Rhineland in 1096 and the brutal sack of Jerusalem in 1099. The sack of Constantinople in 1204, a Christian city attacked by Christian armies, shows how mixed the motives were: money and power were everywhere. Estimates of total deaths run from about one million to several million, and they are very uncertain because medieval records are thin and disease killed as many as swords did.

### The Albigensian Crusade (1209 to 1229)

The Church called a crusade against the Cathars in southern France, a Christian group it considered heretical. The massacre at Béziers in 1209 is the best-known event. Death tolls vary enormously in the sources, from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand, and historians still argue about the numbers. What is clear is that a whole regional culture was crushed, and the Inquisition was created partly as a result.

### The Inquisitions (from the 1200s)

The most famous is the Spanish Inquisition, starting in 1478. The numbers here are lower than the legend. Modern historians estimate about 150,000 people were tried and about 3,000 to 5,000 were executed over centuries. That is still thousands of human beings burned for what they believed. And the damage was larger than the death count: fear, torture, informers, families ruined, books burned, Jews and Muslims forced to convert or leave.

### The witch trials (about 1450 to 1750)

Across Europe, roughly 100,000 people were prosecuted for witchcraft and about 40,000 to 60,000 were executed, most of them women, many of them old, poor, or simply unpopular with a neighbour. Not every trial was run by the Church; many were local and secular courts acting on religious belief. The same pattern appears today in parts of Africa, India, and Papua New Guinea, where people, again mostly women, are still killed on accusations of witchcraft.

### The French Wars of Religion (1562 to 1598)

Catholics and Protestants (Huguenots) fought eight wars in France. The St. Bartholomew's Day massacre in August 1572 began in Paris and spread to other cities. Thousands were killed in Paris alone and the total across France is commonly given as several thousand to tens of thousands. The wars as a whole, including famine and disease, are estimated to have cost about two to four million lives. Politics and noble rivalry were mixed in, but the fault line was religion.

### The Thirty Years' War (1618 to 1648)

This one started as a Catholic and Protestant conflict inside the Holy Roman Empire and grew into a general European war, with France, a Catholic country, fighting on the Protestant side for pure power reasons. Deaths are estimated at 4.5 to 8 million, mostly from famine and plague that followed the armies. Some parts of Germany lost a third of their population. This is a good example of "mixed": religion lit the fire, but state ambition kept feeding it.

### The Taiping Rebellion (1850 to 1864)

Hong Xiuquan, who believed he was the younger brother of Jesus, led a movement in China built on his own reading of Christianity. The war is often listed as one of the deadliest in history, with estimates of 20 million and sometimes much higher. Poverty, anger at the Qing dynasty, and famine were deeply involved, so I would call this mixed rather than purely religious.

## South Asia: the one close to home

### Partition of India, 1947

When British India was divided into India and Pakistan, communal violence exploded. Around 12 to 20 million people were displaced, one of the largest forced migrations in history, and deaths are most often estimated at about one million, with serious scholars giving a range from 200,000 to 2 million. The division was political, but the killing lines ran along religious identity: Hindus and Sikhs against Muslims, and the reverse.

### Direct Action Day, Calcutta, 1946

A call for protest turned into several days of riots in August 1946. Estimates are generally in the range of 4,000 to 10,000 dead. It is often called the beginning of the worst phase that led to Partition.

### The anti-Sikh riots, 1984

After the assassination of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, mobs attacked Sikhs, mostly in Delhi. Official counts are around 3,000 deaths and independent groups say more. The inquiry history that followed, with decades of delay, is part of the story.

### Mumbai, 1992 to 1993

After the demolition of the Babri Masjid in December 1992, riots broke out in Mumbai, followed by the serial bombings of March 1993. Deaths in the riots are usually given as about 900, and the bombings killed roughly 250 more.

### Gujarat, 2002

The Godhra train fire killed 59 people, and the violence that followed across Gujarat killed, by official figures, about 1,000 people, most of them Muslims, with other estimates higher. Courts and commissions have reached different conclusions on different aspects, which is why I only give the widely reported numbers here.

### Terror attacks

Mumbai, November 2008 (the 26/11 attacks): about 166 people killed. These are only a few examples of a longer list across South Asia.

## Europe and the Middle East in the modern era

### Northern Ireland, "The Troubles" (1968 to 1998)

About 3,500 people were killed. The conflict was between a mostly Protestant, pro-British community and a mostly Catholic, nationalist one. Honestly, the real argument was about land, power, jobs, and national identity, and religion was the label on the teams. This is a good example of why "if there were no religion" does not simply remove a conflict: the identity would likely have found another name.

### The Bosnian war (1992 to 1995)

Roughly 100,000 people were killed, including about 8,000 men and boys murdered at Srebrenica in 1995. Ethnic nationalism drove the war, but the groups were largely defined by religion: Orthodox Serbs, Catholic Croats, Muslim Bosniaks. Again, mixed.

### The Armenian genocide (1915 to 1923)

Estimates of deaths range from about 600,000 to 1.5 million. The motives were mainly ethnic and political (a collapsing empire, fear of a Christian minority seen as disloyal), but religious identity decided who was targeted.

### ISIS and the Yazidis (2014)

When ISIS attacked the Yazidi community in Sinjar in August 2014, thousands of men were killed, and thousands of women and girls were abducted and enslaved. Estimates of those killed start at around 5,000, and more than 6,000 were taken. This one was driven primarily by religious ideology.

### Boko Haram (since 2009)

An insurgency in northeast Nigeria and neighbouring countries that has killed tens of thousands and displaced millions, including the 2014 kidnapping of the Chibok schoolgirls. It mixes extremist religious ideology with local grievance and poverty.

### Attacks that shocked the world

- **11 September 2001:** 2,977 victims in the United States, and the wars that followed cost many times more lives.
- **Sri Lanka, Easter 2019:** coordinated bombings of churches and hotels killed about 270 people.

### Jonestown, 1978

A cult leader led 918 people, including hundreds of children, to their deaths in Guyana. A small event in numbers, but a good reminder that faith placed in one man can be deadly even outside any big religion.

## The deaths that are not about war

This is the part people forget, because it does not look like violence. It is quieter.

- **Crowd disasters at religious gatherings.** The 2015 crush at Mina during the Hajj killed at least 769 people by the official count, and other counts say over 2,000. In India, stampedes at religious events happen again and again: a 2024 stampede at a religious gathering in Hathras killed more than 100 people. Nobody wanted these deaths, but crowds of devotees in unsafe places keep dying.
- **Medical refusal.** Children and adults have died when families chose prayer over treatment, or refused blood transfusions or vaccines on religious grounds. No one has a trustworthy worldwide number, and I will not invent one.
- **Practices that harm women and girls.** Child marriage, honour killings, sati in earlier centuries, and restrictions on contraception and women's healthcare all have religious justifications in some communities. Their death toll is real but impossible to separate cleanly from wider culture and poverty.
- **Deaths of people for leaving a faith.** Apostasy and blasphemy laws still exist in a number of countries, and mobs have killed people over accusations alone.

## Now, the other side of the ledger, which we must not skip

If we are honest about religion, we have to be honest about its absence as well.

The twentieth century gave us the largest killing machines in history, and the most terrible of them were not religious in the usual sense.

- **World War II:** about 70 to 85 million dead, driven by nationalism, racism, and imperial ambition. The Holocaust murdered about six million Jews, and it grew out of a racial ideology, though it fed on centuries of religious antisemitism.
- **Stalin's Soviet Union:** the Great Purge of 1937 to 1938 alone saw hundreds of thousands executed, followed by the Gulag and famines. The state was officially atheist and persecuted believers.
- **Mao's China:** the Great Leap Forward famine is estimated to have killed anywhere from 15 to 55 million people, with 30 million a common middle estimate. Religion was suppressed.
- **The Khmer Rouge in Cambodia:** about 1.5 to 2 million dead, around a quarter of the population, in just four years. Buddhism and Islam were both attacked.

I am not saying this to defend religion. I am saying it because it matters for our question. These regimes show that removing religion does not remove cruelty. When a state decides that an *idea* is more important than people, whether that idea is God, the nation, the race, or the Revolution, people become expendable. The common danger is not belief in God. It is **certainty combined with power**.

## So what is the answer to the question?

Let me try to answer it as honestly as I can, and I will show my working.

If I add up the events above where religion was central, the Crusades, the Albigensian Crusade, the Inquisitions, the witch trials and the French Wars of Religion, I get a figure somewhere in the range of 4 to 8 million deaths over roughly 600 years. If I generously add the "mixed" cases, the Thirty Years' War, Partition, the Armenian genocide, Bosnia, the Troubles, and the Indian riots, the range grows to maybe 10 to 20 million or more. These are my own rough sums of other people's estimates, so please treat them as a sense of scale and not as a fact.

Then comes the discount. In many of those cases, such as Partition, Bosnia, and the Troubles, there is a good chance a large share of the violence would have happened anyway under another label: language, tribe, class, nation. Humans have always found a flag to fight under. So the number of lives "saved by no religion" is somewhere between a few million and a few tens of millions across a thousand years, and, honestly, nobody can pin it down.

For comparison, the twentieth century's ideological regimes alone killed far more people in a few decades than most of the religious violence of a thousand years. That should humble all of us, whatever we believe.

One more thing belongs in a fair account. Religion has also saved lives: hospitals, orphanages, famine relief, and disaster response run by religious groups, and individuals who risked everything to hide their neighbours from killers because their faith told them to. I cannot give you a number for this either, and I will not pretend to. The truth is that religion is a very powerful tool, and tools can build or break.

## What actually saves lives

If the goal is fewer deaths, the evidence points to a short list of things that work, regardless of what anyone believes:

1. **Rule of law.** Equal courts that protect minorities, and punish mobs and leaders who incite them, whoever they are.
2. **Education.** Especially science, history taught honestly, and critical thinking, so people can question a rumour before they act on it.
3. **Equality for women and girls.** Societies where women have rights and healthcare are safer for everybody.
4. **Neutral institutions.** A state that treats all citizens the same, whatever their faith or lack of it.
5. **Healthcare and basic security.** Fear and poverty make people easy to recruit for violence.
6. **Free speech and the freedom to doubt.** Both believing and not believing must be safe. A society where you can leave your religion or join one without fear is one where fewer people die for ideas.

## A last word

The question "what if there was no religion?" is a natural one, and I understand why it comes up when you read about these events. My own view, which I have said on this site before, is that what we should get rid of is not faith or the lack of it, but **hatred, violence, and the belief that our group is worth more than another person's life**. Believers and non-believers have both failed this test, and both have also passed it.

The most useful thing any of us can do is simple and not easy: refuse to hate, refuse to join the mob, and treat the person in front of us as a human being first. That would save more lives than any argument about God ever has.

If you notice a number in this article that you think is wrong, please write to me through the Contact page. I would rather correct it than leave it.
