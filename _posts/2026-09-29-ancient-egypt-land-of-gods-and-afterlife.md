Few civilizations have fascinated people as much as ancient Egypt. Its pyramids still stand, its mummies still look back at us from museum cases, and its gods, with the heads of falcons, jackals and cats, are known to children all over the world. But behind the golden masks there was something even more remarkable: a whole way of seeing life, death and the universe that lasted for about three thousand years.

That is longer than the time between us and the Roman Empire. Let us take a walk through it.

## A gift of the Nile

Egypt is mostly desert. Life exists there because of one river. Every year the Nile flooded, left a layer of rich black soil, and then withdrew, and farmers planted their crops in it. The Egyptians called their fertile land *Kemet*, the "Black Land", and the desert around it *Deshret*, the "Red Land".

This rhythm shaped their religion. The sun rose every morning in the east and set every evening in the west. The river rose and fell. Life came out of the dark soil each year. To the Egyptians, the world was a place of cycles, and the gods were the forces that kept the cycles going.

## Many gods, many faces

Egyptian religion had no single holy book and no single founder. It grew over centuries, and different cities had their own favorite gods. Some of the best known were:

| God | What they were linked to |
|---|---|
| Ra | The sun, and later the king of the gods |
| Osiris | Death, resurrection and the afterlife |
| Isis | Magic, motherhood and protection |
| Horus | The sky and kingship |
| Set | Storms, chaos and the desert |
| Anubis | Embalming and the care of the dead |
| Thoth | Writing, wisdom and the moon |
| Hathor | Love, music and joy |
| Amun | The hidden god, later joined with Ra as Amun-Ra |

The gods often had animal features, such as a falcon, jackal, ibis or cow. This did not mean the Egyptians thought their gods were animals. Animals were used as symbols of a quality: the sharp eyes of the falcon, the watchfulness of the jackal near graveyards. Egyptians were also comfortable with the same god showing up in different forms, or with two gods merging, as Amun and Ra did.

One of the most famous stories is about Osiris. According to the myth, his brother Set killed him and cut up his body. Isis gathered the pieces, brought him back to life long enough to have a son, Horus, and Osiris became the king of the dead. Horus later defeated Set. For the Egyptians, this story explained both the rule of kings and the hope of life after death.

## The pharaoh: a bridge between gods and people

The king, whom we call the pharaoh, was not just a ruler. He was believed to be the living Horus and the son of Ra, the person who stood between the gods and the people. His job was to keep the world in balance.

That balance had a name: **Ma'at**. Ma'at means truth, justice, order and harmony. It was also a goddess, shown with an ostrich feather on her head. A good pharaoh upheld Ma'at. A bad harvest, a lost war or a flood that did not come could be read as a sign that Ma'at was in danger.

## The afterlife and the weighing of the heart

Death, for the Egyptians, was not the end. It was a dangerous journey. The most famous scene appears in the *Book of the Dead*, a collection of spells written on scrolls and placed in tombs during the New Kingdom (about 1550 to 1070 BCE).

The dead person entered a hall where their heart was placed on one side of a scale. On the other side was the feather of Ma'at. If the heart was light, meaning the person had lived honestly, they could go on to a peaceful life in the fields of the afterlife. If the heart was heavy with wrongdoing, a creature called Ammit, part crocodile, part lion, part hippopotamus, devoured it, and the person ceased to exist.

Before the scale, the dead person had to declare a long list of things they had not done: "I have not stolen, I have not killed, I have not caused tears." Historians call this the *negative confession*. It shows something important. Egyptian religion was not only about ritual and magic, it was also about how you lived.

## Mummies: keeping the body for the journey

Because the Egyptians believed the person needed a body in the afterlife, they developed the art of mummification. The process took around seventy days. Embalmers removed the organs, drew the brain out through the nose, dried the body with natron salt, and wrapped it in linen with amulets tucked between the layers. The liver, lungs, stomach and intestines were stored in special jars. The heart was normally left in the body, because it was needed for the judgment.

At first only kings were mummified. Later, wealthier people could afford it too, and eventually many ordinary Egyptians, and even sacred animals such as cats and ibises, were mummified.

## Pyramids and tombs

The great pyramids of Giza were built during the Old Kingdom, around 2600 to 2500 BCE, as tombs for the pharaohs Khufu, Khafre and Menkaure. Khufu's pyramid, the largest, stood as the tallest man-made structure in the world for nearly four thousand years.

Inside the pyramid of King Unas and several other Old Kingdom pyramids you can find the **Pyramid Texts**, carved around 2400 to 2300 BCE. They are among the oldest religious writings we have. Later, in the New Kingdom, kings were buried in hidden tombs in the Valley of the Kings instead, including Tutankhamun, whose almost untouched tomb was found by Howard Carter in 1922.

## The king who tried to change everything

Around 1353 BCE, a pharaoh named Amenhotep IV did something extraordinary. He changed his name to **Akhenaten**, moved his capital to a new city called Akhetaten (now Amarna), and promoted the worship of a single sun disk, the **Aten**, above all other gods.

Some people call this the world's first attempt at monotheism, but historians are careful. Akhenaten did not always deny the other gods outright, and the king himself was the main link to the Aten. What we do know is that it upset many priests and traditions. After his death, the old gods were restored, his city was abandoned, and later rulers tried to erase his name from the records.

## What about ordinary people?

Most Egyptians never entered the great temples, which were considered the homes of the gods and were run by priests. Ordinary people prayed at home shrines, wore protective amulets and honored family ancestors. They asked local gods for help with everyday things, such as protection for a child in childbirth. Two popular household gods were Bes, a comical dwarf-like figure who protected families, and Taweret, a hippo-shaped goddess who guarded mothers.

Festivals mattered too. During the Opet festival at Thebes, for example, statues of gods were carried out of the temple in procession, and crowds gathered to see them.

## How the religion ended

After Alexander the Great conquered Egypt in 332 BCE, Greek rulers governed the country, and later Rome did. The old religion did not die at once. The goddess Isis became so popular that her worship spread across the Roman Empire, as far as Britain.

But as Christianity grew, the old temples lost their support. Around the end of the 4th century CE, Roman emperors banned pagan worship. The last known hieroglyphic inscription, at the temple of Philae, dates to 394 CE. Slowly, the ability to read hieroglyphs was lost as well, until a stone found by French soldiers in 1799, the **Rosetta Stone**, helped Jean-François Champollion crack the code in 1822.

## What we still do not know

It is easy to make ancient Egypt sound neat and complete. It was not. Most of what we know comes from tombs and temples, which tell us about kings and priests more than about farmers and craftsmen. The meaning of many rituals is still debated, and dates keep shifting by decades as new evidence appears. The number of gods we can name is enormous, and they changed over three thousand years.

## Why it still matters

Ancient Egypt shows how deeply people have tried to answer the questions we still ask: What happens when we die? How should we live? What keeps the world in order? Their answers included magic and gods with animal faces, but also a serious idea that a person's life is measured by honesty and fairness.

It also shows how even the most powerful religions can fade. A faith that ruled a great civilization for thirty centuries slowly disappeared, and today its gods live on in museums, books and stories.
