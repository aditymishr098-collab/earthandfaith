It is 2026. India sends rockets to the Moon, and its women run companies, courts and research labs. Yet walk into almost any town and you will still find millions of women who believe that Krishna, Ram or another deity will personally protect them and their families. And in recent decades a new figure has joined the old gods: the living "avatar", the godman who claims to be a divine incarnation, sometimes even Kalki, the tenth avatar of Vishnu whom the tradition places in the future.

This is a sensitive subject, so let me say clearly what this post is and is not. It is a critical look at ideas, institutions and history. It is not an attack on Indian women. The question I want to ask is not "why are women so gullible?" but a harder one: **why has religion asked so much of women, and given so little protection in return?**

## Why the belief in a divine protector is so strong

Faith rarely survives on argument alone. It survives because it answers a real need. For many Indian women, the need is safety. Roads can be dangerous, the police are often hard to reach, the courts are slow, and a family's honor is still too often placed on a woman's shoulders. When the world feels unsafe and institutions feel distant, a god who always listens and never turns you away is a powerful comfort.

Religion also gives women things that are easy to overlook: community, a language for grief, festivals, and moments of respect. Fasts, songs and temple visits are, for many women, the one place where they lead rather than follow. Surveys, including large studies by Pew Research Center, show that religion is very important to most Indians, and women are as devout as men or more so. If we want to criticize the beliefs, we should begin by understanding why they matter.

## The rise of the living avatar

The belief in miracles has always been part of Indian religious life. What is new is the business around it. Over the past few decades, several men have presented themselves as avatars, saints or even the promised Kalki. They draw huge crowds, and many of their most loyal followers are women.

Some of these stories have ended in courtrooms. Asaram Bapu was convicted in 2018 of raping a teenage girl who came to him for spiritual help, and Gurmeet Ram Rahim Singh was convicted in 2017 of raping two of his female followers. These are not just headlines about "bad apples". They raise a structural question: what happens when a person is believed to be a god, and nobody is allowed to question him?

To be fair, most spiritual teachers are not criminals, and it would be wrong to say they are. But the pattern is worth naming. Where devotion is total and doubt is treated as a sin, abuse becomes very hard to see, and even harder to report.

## A history that came at a cost to women

To understand today, it helps to look at how religion and custom have treated women in the past.

**Sati.** For centuries in parts of India, widows were sometimes burned on their husbands' funeral pyres. Some were forced, some seem to have been pressured, and some may have chosen it under the weight of a society that offered widows almost nothing. The British banned the practice in Bengal in 1829, after a long campaign by Indian reformers, especially Raja Ram Mohan Roy. Yet the Roop Kanwar case in Rajasthan in 1987 showed that the idea had not disappeared, and led to a new law.

**Dress and caste in Travancore.** In the 19th century, in parts of what is now Kerala, women of certain lower castes were expected not to cover their upper bodies in front of higher castes. Women who resisted faced violence, and the struggle, often called the Channar revolt or Upper Cloth agitation, lasted decades before reforms in the 1850s and 1860s. It is important to be exact here. This was a rule of caste hierarchy, enforced by custom and local rulers, not a rule imposed on all women in the same way. Some popular stories about it, like the "breast tax", are better treated as legend than proven fact by careful historians. The restrictions on dress themselves are well documented.

**Tests of purity.** In the Ramayana, Sita is asked to prove her purity by walking through fire, while no such test is asked of Ram. Whatever the story means to devotees, generations of women have been taught to see her as the ideal, and that ideal is one of endurance.

## What the texts say, and what is still debated

People often quote scripture to show how religion views women. This needs care, because the texts are large and contradictory, and believers read them in very different ways.

Some passages in the Manusmriti tell women to remain under the protection of father, husband and son, while others speak of honoring women. A verse in Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas, often quoted online, lists women alongside drums, animals and others as things to be "disciplined". Defenders say the line is spoken by a character in the story, not the author, and that the key word can be read differently. Critics say that even if it is debated, it has been used for centuries to justify control.

I think the honest position is this: many texts and traditions contain deeply patriarchal ideas, and many others contain powerful women and gentler voices. The question is which ones society chose to amplify.

## The double standard

Then there are the stories we treasure. The Bhagavata Purana describes Krishna with thousands of wives, and devotees explain it as a symbol of divine love, not a household model. That may be so. But an ordinary man is not judged by such symbols. A woman is usually expected to be devoted to one husband, and any suspicion of her loyalty can cost her dearly. Men's freedom is treated as normal, and women's freedom as danger. That double standard is not from the sky, it has been taught and repeated by families, sermons and films.

## It is not the whole story

If I stopped here, I would be telling only half the truth, so let me add the rest.

- **Women have always challenged the system from inside religion.** Mirabai, Akka Mahadevi, Andal and Lal Ded were women poets and saints who spoke about God on their own terms.
- **Reformers like Savitribai Phule and Pandita Ramabai** fought for girls' education and widows' rights in the 19th century, often at great personal cost.
- **Courts and law have moved.** In 2018 the Supreme Court ruled that women of all ages could enter the Sabarimala temple, although the matter was later referred to a larger bench and remains contested. Earlier, in 2016, the Bombay High Court supported women's entry to the Haji Ali dargah and the Shani Shingnapur temple.
- **This is not only a Hindu story.** Every major religion has struggled with the place of women, and readers of other faiths could write similar posts about their own traditions.
- **Devout women are not fools.** Many practice their faith with full awareness, and many use it as a source of strength and independence. Respecting that does not mean agreeing with every belief.

## What could change things

Three things seem to matter most. First, **education and economic independence**, since a woman who can support herself has more freedom to question anything. Second, **institutions that work**: safe cities, fast courts and police who take women seriously, so that women do not need to depend on divine protection alone. Third, a culture of **asking questions about texts and teachers**, including the polite but firm question: "Where is the evidence?"

## A last thought

Belief is not a weakness of women or of Indians. It is a human need, and it will not disappear because someone writes a blog post. But every belief should be free to be questioned, and every believer should be free to walk away from it without fear. That is the freedom I want for all people, women especially.
